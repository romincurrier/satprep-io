SAT GRAMMAR & PUNCTUATION

Find the sentence structure first; choose the punctuation or verb second.

College Board’s current Standard English Conventions domain includes Boundaries and Form, Structure, and Sense. These questions test whether a sentence is built and punctuated according to Standard English—not whether one option merely “sounds right.”

A structure-first process for SAT grammar

  1. Find the sentence core. Identify the true subject, main verb, and complete clause or clauses.
  2. Ignore interrupting phrases temporarily. Prepositional phrases and modifiers can distract you from subject-verb agreement or clause boundaries.
  3. Classify each side of the blank. Is it a complete independent clause, a dependent clause, a phrase, or a list?
  4. Choose the rule that matches the structure. Punctuation and verb form should follow the grammatical relationship.
  5. Reread for meaning. A grammatically possible answer can still distort the intended sentence.

Boundaries: ask whether the ideas can stand alone

Two complete clauses

A period, semicolon, or an appropriate coordinating structure may join or separate complete clauses. A comma alone cannot normally join two independent clauses.

Complete clause followed by explanation or list

A colon can introduce a list or explanation when the words before it form a complete clause.

Essential vs. nonessential information

Commas, dashes, or parentheses can set off nonessential material, but essential modifiers should not be separated in a way that breaks the sentence’s meaning.

Common trap: choosing punctuation based on the length of the pause you hear. SAT punctuation is determined by grammatical structure, not breathing.

Form, Structure, and Sense: identify what controls the form

Agreement questions often hide the true subject behind a nearby plural noun. Verb tense questions require consistency with the time relationship in the sentence or passage. Pronoun and modifier questions depend on clear reference and placement. In each case, identify the word or relationship that actually controls the choice before comparing options.

Use elimination by rule

When answer choices differ mainly in punctuation or grammatical form, avoid reading each option as an entirely new sentence. Determine the structure once, then eliminate choices that violate that structure. This is faster and more reliable than relying on sound alone.

Separate grammar evidence from other Reading & Writing skills

A student can be strong at inference and evidence yet repeatedly miss clause-boundary questions. Skill-level tracking allows the learning plan to address the exact convention instead of assigning generic reading practice.
