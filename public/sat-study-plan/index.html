SAT STUDY PLAN

A good SAT study plan should change when the student changes.

Generic calendars are easy to print, but they often spend equal time on unequal needs. A stronger plan begins with evidence, identifies specific skill priorities, teaches those skills, measures what changed, and then updates the next round of work.

Five steps for building a focused SAT plan

1. Establish the baseline

Use available prior testing and a fresh diagnostic to identify current strengths and gaps. A diagnostic should measure first; teaching and explanations belong in practice sessions afterward.

2. Define the target

Record the target exam, target score, and expected test date. The goal provides direction, but skill evidence should determine what gets studied first.

3. Prioritize skills

Break broad Math and Reading and Writing results into specific skills. Work first on gaps that are both meaningful and currently weak.

4. Learn, practice, review

Teach the process, answer targeted questions, review the correct answer and explanation, and repeat until the method is dependable.

5. Reassess and adjust

Use new practice evidence to change priorities. Skills that improve should receive less attention; persistent weaknesses should receive more instruction or a different explanation.

What should a weekly SAT study schedule include?

The exact number of sessions depends on the student's timeline, current level, school workload, and available time. The structure matters more than forcing every student into the same number of hours.

  • Focused skill sessions: one or two high-priority skills at a time.
  • Mixed practice: questions from several domains after individual skills become more reliable.
  • Error review: time devoted to understanding why an answer was wrong and how to solve the problem correctly next time.
  • Timed practice: added progressively so the student learns to use reliable skills under realistic pacing.
  • Periodic reassessment: enough fresh evidence to determine whether the plan should change.

Why an adaptive plan can use study time more efficiently

If a student consistently demonstrates mastery of one skill, spending the same amount of time on it as a weak skill is inefficient. Adaptive preparation uses performance evidence to redirect limited study time. It does not guarantee a particular score; it creates a more defensible reason for what the student studies next.

Example: a student whose prior report and diagnostic both flag inference but show strong geometry should receive more inference instruction initially. If later practice shows inference improving and another weakness emerging, the plan should shift.

Use official-format practice at the right time

The current digital SAT has two sections—Reading and Writing and Math—and uses a two-stage adaptive design. Skill-focused preparation should eventually be combined with official College Board practice so students experience the real interface, timing, and adaptive test structure before test day.
