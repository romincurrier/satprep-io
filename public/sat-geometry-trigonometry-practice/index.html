SAT GEOMETRY & TRIGONOMETRY

Draw the relationships first; choose the formula second.

College Board’s current SAT Math specifications include area and volume, lines and angles, triangles, right-triangle trigonometry, and circles in Geometry and Trigonometry. The strongest approach is to identify the geometric relationship before reaching for a formula.

What SAT Geometry and Trigonometry practice should cover

Area and volume

Translate diagrams and descriptions into dimensions, choose the correct measurement, and keep linear, square, and cubic units distinct.

Lines, angles, and triangles

Use angle relationships, triangle sums, similarity, congruence, and proportional reasoning. Mark known values on the figure before calculating.

Right triangles and trigonometry

Recognize Pythagorean relationships, special right triangles, and sine/cosine/tangent relationships. Identify the angle and sides before choosing a ratio.

Circles

Work with radius, diameter, circumference, area, arcs, angles, and equations of circles. Keep radius and diameter from being interchanged accidentally.

A reusable geometry-question process

  1. Label the diagram. Add known lengths, angles, parallel lines, radii, or right-angle marks.
  2. Identify the relationship. Decide whether the problem is about similarity, angle rules, area, volume, a right triangle, or a circle relationship.
  3. Write the formula or theorem with symbols first. This reduces substitution mistakes.
  4. Substitute carefully and preserve units. Square or cube measurements only when the quantity requires it.
  5. Check whether the answer is plausible. An area smaller than one of its dimensions or an angle outside a possible range can signal an error.
Common trap: solving for a radius when the question asks for a diameter—or using the diameter as though it were the radius in a circle formula.

Right-triangle questions start with orientation

For trigonometric ratios, identify the reference angle first. Then label the opposite, adjacent, and hypotenuse sides relative to that angle. The same side can be “opposite” for one acute angle and “adjacent” for the other, which is why memorizing a ratio without orienting the triangle can lead to errors.

Geometry rewards visual checks

The diagram may not always be drawn to scale, but geometric constraints still provide useful checks. Triangle angles must sum to 180 degrees, a hypotenuse must be the longest side of a right triangle, and a diameter must be twice the radius. Those relationships can catch arithmetic mistakes before a student commits to an answer.

Target the exact geometry skill that needs work

A broad Math score can hide very different profiles: one student may need circle practice while another needs right-triangle reasoning. Skill-level evidence allows the learning plan to prioritize the narrower gap.
